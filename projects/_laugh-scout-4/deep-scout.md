## 1. Dolan v. United States Postal Service, No. 04-848, argued 7 Nov 2005

Sides: James R. Radmore for Barbara Dolan (petitioner, the homeowner); Patricia A. Millett, Assistant to the Solicitor General, for the Postal Service. Oyez has the argument audio and an opinion announcement (22 Feb 2006). supremecourt.gov has no audio for this term.

### a. Story
1. A postal carrier leaves mail on Barbara Dolan's porch: "letters, packages, and periodicals" (Thomas dissent).
2. The mail "allegedly caused her to trip and fall", and she claims injuries.
3. She sues the Postal Service under the Federal Tort Claims Act for negligently placing the mail.
4. Two courts throw it out: the law's exception for "negligent transmission" of mail, they say, covers it.
5. The Supreme Court disagrees, 7 to 1: she can sue.

- **Open on:** the carrier dumping a pile of parcels on the porch, and a foot catching on it. (Scalia's words, 9:13-15: "just dumping the stuff there on the porch... the homeowner tripped over it".)
- **Hold back:** the government's rule means a mail truck that hits you can be sued but a parcel that trips you, or skis sticking out of a mail truck, can't ("Yes, that is our position", 33:12). Then the reveal that the Post Office lost.

### b. The one argument: is leaving mail on a porch "negligent transmission"?
1. **Stakes**, 3:10-3:16, Radmore (Dolan): "The Federal Tort Claims Act's postal-matter exception bars any claim arising out of the failure of the Postal Service to fulfill its duty to deliver mail to its intended destination on time and in good condition, but does not bar any claim arising out of ordinary negligence that happens to occur while the tortfeasor is delivering mail."
2. **The grilling** (a justice pressing the Postal Service's lawyer), 43:18-44:15, Chief Justice Roberts to Millett: "But if the postman throws the box onto the porch and hits somebody there, you say that's within the exception?" ... "But you don't say, "Hey, you hit me with the mail," right? You say, "You hit me with a box."" ... "when you trip over something -- a box -- you don't care whether it's a mail box or if it's the box from UPS. And yet, in your case, it makes all the difference in the world."
3. **The absurd extreme**, 33:1-33:14, Breyer to Millett: "you have a driver of the truck, the postal truck, and what he does is, he puts the mail in the truck, so my skis, which I have shipped by mail, happen to stick out the side, and, as he walk -- drives along, he just mows down the pedestrians." (Laughter.) "I take it, on your theory, that that -- there is no lawsuit." Millett: "Yes, that is our position. If he mows 'em down with his arm or with his rearview mirror on his truck, your -- the liability attaches." The opinion picks up this exact anomaly (the side-view mirror versus a dangling parcel).
4. **Who it hurts**, 56:3-56:15, Roberts to the petitioner's side in rebuttal: "you'll be responsible, if you prevail, for all of us having to go down to the Post Office every time we get --" (Laughter.) "-- packages." The reply at 56:13 ("Then I'll probably be subject to some intentional torts, myself") is labelled MS. MILLETT in the transcript but comes during Radmore's rebuttal: check the voice. The government's side of the harm: Millett, 33:15-33:22, "The Postal Service is defenseless."
5. **The answer**, 58:11-58:14, Radmore: "according to the Government, there's only been ten a year. So, I don't think that there's a real concern by the Post Office that there's going to be some -- a floodgate of litigation opened up."

### d. Sendable ending
"If the post leaves a parcel where you trip over it, you can take the Post Office to court."
Holding (Kennedy, for the Court, 7-1; Thomas dissenting, Alito not taking part): "We disagree and hold that Dolan's suit may proceed." 546 U.S. 481, 483. And: "Slip-and-fall liability, however, to the extent state tort law imposes it, is a risk shared by any business that makes home deliveries." (at 491). The ruling lets her sue; it doesn't say she wins.

### e. Roles on the audio
Eight justices (Roberts, O'Connor, Scalia, Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, Ginsburg, Breyer); the two lawyers; the homeowner; the postman, letter carrier or mail carrier; pedestrians; a postal truck driver; the grocery boy; stagecoach drivers; an IRS agent; FedEx, UPS, pizza and department-store delivery people. Objects: packages, boxes, mail sacks, letters, a cellophane envelope, a toupee, skis, live alligators, a mail truck and its rearview mirror, the porch, the mailbox, the Post Office counter.

### f. Risks
- No swearing.
- Brands said aloud: UPS (inside the grilling, 44:14), FedEx, Domino's, McDonald's. Draw generic boxes and vans.
- Sensitive: anthrax, letter bombs and biohazards (26:18; 53:24-54:8); a hurricane reference (27:6-7). All in CUT stretches.
- Her injury is never described. Show a trip and fall, nothing more. The negligence was only alleged.

## 2. Kentucky v. King, No. 09-1272, argued 12 Jan 2011

Sides: Joshua D. Farley for Kentucky (petitioner, the police side); Ann O'Connell for the United States supporting Kentucky; Jamesa J. Drake for Hollis King (respondent). supremecourt.gov has the argument audio; Oyez has an opinion announcement (16 May 2011).

### a. Story
1. Lexington police run a controlled crack buy; the undercover officer radios uniformed officers to hurry before the suspect gets into an apartment.
2. In the breezeway they hear a door shut and smell burnt marijuana at the left-hand door.
3. They bang on it, shout "Police", and hear movement inside.
4. They kick the door in and find King and two others with marijuana and cocaine. Kentucky's top court throws the evidence out: the police should have foreseen that knocking would make people destroy it.
5. The suspect they were chasing was behind the right-hand door all along.

- **Open on:** officers sprinting into the breezeway, a fist hammering on the door.
- **Hold back:** the wrong door.

### b. The one argument: if lawful knocking makes people flush the evidence, can police then kick the door in?
1. **Stakes**, 5:9-5:12, Farley: "as long as an officer behaves lawfully, there should be no suppression of evidence seized after an otherwise reasonable search."
2. **The grilling**, 34:4-35:5, Scalia to Drake (King's lawyer): "the one thing that -- that it has going for it is that criminals are stupid." (Laughter.) ... "These people could have answered the door ... and say "Yes, what do you want?" ... They say "Well, get a warrant." Shut the door. They didn't do that." Drake, 35:6-35:14: "there is no difference between what happened in this case and how an innocent person would respond... Any innocent person at 10:00 at night would have to move". The police side is grilled at 13:9-13:24 (Sotomayor: "Why do we even bother giving them a -- a warrant?").
3. **The absurd extreme**, 5:13-5:21, Roberts: police "walk through and knock on every door and wait for evidence of the destruction of -- of drugs. Is that all right?" Farley: "I would say "yes," if there's probable cause as well as exigent circumstances."
4. **Who it hurts**, 52:9-52:17, Kagan: "all the police really have to say is: We saw pot, we heard noise."
5. **The answer**, 51:15-51:19, Farley: "Can lawful police action impermissibly create exigent circumstances? And the answer to that question is no."

### d. Sendable ending
"You don't have to open the door to the police. But if you start flushing things, they can kick it in."
Holding (Alito, 8-1; Ginsburg dissenting): "For these reasons, we conclude that the exigent circumstances rule applies when the police do not gain entry to premises by means of an actual or threatened violation of the Fourth Amendment." 563 U.S. 452.

### e. Roles
Eight justices (Thomas silent); three lawyers; police officers (Officer Cobb, Detective Maynard, Sergeant Simmons); the fleeing suspect; occupants; drug "mules" on buses; an innocent neighbour; a magistrate. Objects: doors, a toilet, a breezeway, a warrant, marijuana smoke, a pizza.

### f. Risks
No swearing ("Oh, heck, no" at 14:18). No brands. Drugs throughout (marijuana, crack, cocaine), discussed dryly. Scalia's "criminals are stupid" mocks suspects; King is a real private person who got 11 years. The tape ends with a reference to the memorial after the January 2011 Tucson shooting (55:15-55:20): cut it.

## 3. City of Ontario v. Quon, No. 08-1332, argued 19 Apr 2010

Sides: Kent L. Richland for the city (petitioners); Neal Katyal, Deputy Solicitor General, for the United States supporting the city; Dieter Dammeier for Quon and the others (respondents). Oyez has the audio and an opinion announcement (17 Jun 2010). supremecourt.gov has no audio for this term.

### a. Story
1. The city gives its SWAT team text pagers; its written policy says users "should have no expectation of privacy".
2. Sgt. Jeff Quon keeps going over the 25,000-character monthly limit and pays the overage each time, on his lieutenant's word that he won't be audited if he pays.
3. The lieutenant gets "tired of being a bill collector", and the chief orders transcripts of two months of Quon's texts.
4. Many aren't work-related, and some are sexually explicit. The city says Quon was disciplined; he and the people he texted sue.
5. The Supreme Court rules 9-0 for the city.

- **Open on:** the pager buzzing and a character counter racing past 25,000.
- **Hold back:** what the texts were about, and that the Court never decided whether he had any privacy: it assumed he did, and he lost anyway.

### b. The one argument: was reading all the texts a reasonable search?
1. **Stakes**, 18:13-18:21, Katyal (city side): "Millions of employees today use technologies of their -- of their employers... Put most simply, the computer help desk cannot supplant the chief's desk."
2. **The grilling**, 33:17-34:14, Breyer to Dammeier (Quon's lawyer): "if I had a -- like, 20, 30,000 characters in 1,800 messages and I wanted to know which are personal and which are work-related, a good way to get at least a good first cut would be to read them." (Laughter.)
3. **The absurd extreme**, 34:24-35:18, Breyer: "the officers might say: I don't want you to read these messages because they happen to be about the sexual activity of some of my coworkers and their wives and me" ... "So I guess if you had asked for consent, the officer would have said no." (Laughter.)
4. **Who it hurts**, 54:18-55:1, Dammeier: "It's like if I pick up a phone and I'm a public employee and I call my wife, I should be able to have some expectation of privacy... Do they have no private life, now?"
5. **The answer**, 42:15-42:18, Scalia: "They didn't know which ones were private messages, did they?" Dammeier: "Not until they read them." Scalia: "Not until they read them."

## The pick

**Dolan.** It has the most laughs inside the one fight (four moments, seven markers), all built on a parcel on a doorstep, which everyone gets. Its ending is the most sendable of the three and carries no drugs or sex. King has supremecourt.gov audio and a great wrong-door twist, but its laughs lean on "criminals are stupid" and drugs. Quon's best laughs sit on top of other people's sex lives.
