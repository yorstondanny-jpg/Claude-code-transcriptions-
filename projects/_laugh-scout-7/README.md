# Laugh scout 7: October Terms 1980 to 1999 (Oyez), plus the federal appeals courts

Run on 4 Nov 2026 by the PR scout (F207, F213, F214, F217). Pending Review's Reels slid for a week, mostly because the queue ran dry, and the pick is back to 8/10 with the stakes floor. Every Supreme Court term from 2000 to now had been scanned (scouts 4 to 6), so this scout went back further and wider.

## What was scanned

- **Supreme Court, October Terms 1980 to 1999: 2,432 arguments.** supremecourt.gov's transcript list starts at 2000, so this used Oyez's timed transcripts, which mark the room's laughs as "[Laughter]". New tool: `oyez_scan.py` (`python3 oyez_scan.py projects/_laugh-scout-7/scan <first> <last>`; resumes where it stops). Raw data: `scan/oyez.jsonl` and `scan/oyez.json` (every argument, most laughs first, each laugh with its time, speaker and the 300 characters before it). 1990s: 922 arguments, 702 with a laugh. 1980s: 1,510 arguments, far fewer marked laughs (Oyez marked them less often for the 1980s).
- **Federal appeals courts through CourtListener** (the token works): searched case names and transcripts for everyday things (pizza, burgers, dogs, chickens, slack fill, Christmas lights, tyre chalking, hot tubs, footlongs and more). CourtListener's transcripts don't mark laughs, so the best few were transcribed here with timestamps and their pauses ranked by room loudness. An open laughter classifier (YAMNet) was tried and rejected: on the Sturgeon hovercraft argument it missed all six laughs the official transcript marks.
- **State courts:** CourtListener only carries the Illinois Appellate Court among state courts. Its everyday-object hits (a pot-bellied pig, a llama) were an animal's death and a dog bite, so out. Ohio and Nebraska were not reached this run.

How each row was judged: the setup before every laugh was read and classed object/situation or process (as `_laugh-scout-4/README.md` describes), then the case was scored on the playbook rubric (premise, exchange, stakes, payoff, send: 0 to 2 each) with the F213 stakes floor (an ordinary person stands to lose something they'd care about, and the laugh is about the thing). Every row passed `python3 pipeline/candidates.py --check` (exit 0). Two notes on that check: Bond v. United States here is the **2000 bus bag case (98-9349)**, not the 2014 chemical case already made as `bond-doorknob`, so it is listed with "(bus bag, 2000)" in its name; and California v. Acevedo and California v. Carney only soft-match `riley-phone` and `prado-navarette` on the word "California" (different cases).

Audio: every row below is Supreme Court audio from before October Term 2010, so it comes from Oyez (the mp3 link is in `scan/oyez.json`) or the National Archives, logged as the source on the card. Laugh times are Oyez's transcript times, which can be a few seconds off: hear the room before building. Left out as political, sensitive or sad: Term Limits, Boerne, Erie (nude dancing), Zobrest, Santa Fe, Texas v. Johnson, Troxel (the grandparents' son had died), Smith and Muscarello, Clinton v. Jones, Whren, Chicago v. Morales, Nordlinger (a ballot measure).

## What if bank

Arguments where three or more justices' hypotheticals got a laugh. These do not need the stakes floor to bank, but a What if episode still takes one laugh per case.

- Wisconsin Department of Revenue v. Wrigley (91-119, 1992, 15 laughs): chewing gum. Blackmun on how fast gum goes stale, White's samples "your wife doesn't like them in your house", Scalia's taxi full of gum ("If you just have them driven around constantly you'll be all right"), Souter selling gum in the Denver Post. Stakes 1 (a company's tax), so never a main Reel.
- Glickman v. Wileman Brothers (95-1184, 1996, 7): peaches and plums. Rehnquist and Scalia on beer ("You think beer is less important than peaches?"), Scalia's "I would never give my wife a green plum." Stakes 1 (fruit companies).
- Minnesota v. Carter (97-1147, 1998, 9): Justice O'Connor's pizza man ("Most pizza men don't get past the front door"), the babysitter against the drug dealer, venetian against bamboo blinds.
- City of Ladue v. Gilleo (92-1856, 1994, 11): a sign in a homeowner's window. Stevens's colonial costumes, Scalia's "why triangles are worse than rectangles", "Beware of the dog". The sign's own message was about a war, so keep it out of frame.
- Louisiana v. Mississippi (121-orig, 1995, 9): which state owns an island that floods every year. Scalia: "Where were you all when you were in the boat?"
- Knowles v. Iowa and Maryland v. Wilson (rows 3 and 7) also each have three justices' hypotheticals that got a laugh.
